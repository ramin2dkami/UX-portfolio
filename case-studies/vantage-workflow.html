Vantage

Intelligent Media Processing And Workflow Automation

Vantage automates how broadcasters and post houses transcode, analyze and deliver media — but building a workflow meant knowing where everything was hidden. Here's how a heuristic-led redesign turned a power-user tool into one a new engineer could learn.

01

Understanding The Landscape Of The Situation

A mature product with no design function

Vantage's workflow designer had grown one feature request at a time. Core functions sat behind right-clicks and stacked modals that only long-tenured users knew how to reach, and nobody had ever audited the experience.

Leading the redesign

I ran a heuristic evaluation of the shipping product, interviewed customers, built the primary persona and journey map, benchmarked five competitors, then carried the work from information architecture through high-fidelity UI.

Make the power
already there findable

The goal was never to change what Vantage could do. It was to make capability that already existed discoverable by someone opening the designer for the first time, without slowing down the people who had used it for years.

How I made an impact

Six feature areas rebuilt on a modern design system, plus an AI-assisted workflow generation concept that opened a new direction for the product roadmap.

02

Addressing The Core Problems

Actions hidden behind right-clicks

So many core functions of each node were only reachable by right-clicking it. New users had no way to discover what an action could do, or even that the option existed.

Modals stacked on modals

Configuring a single node opened one modal over another. Users lost track of which node they were editing and got pulled away from what they had set out to do.

No way to find anything

There was no search or filtering for workflows or for actions, and no separation between the workflow list and the design canvas. In a domain where a single workflow can run to a hundred actions, that scales badly.

Errors you couldn't diagnose

When a job failed, the system returned a generic failure message. Users had to re-enter edit mode, find the node highlighted in red, and read through every configuration to work out what went wrong.

03

What I Was Up Against

01
Rigid back-end architecture Read more

Each action's configuration UI was generated by a back end that made structural layout changes expensive, so the redesign had to work within the existing shape of every action rather than starting from a blank page.

02
Experts with deep muscle memory Read more

Vantage's power users had built hundreds of workflows over years. Anything that made a familiar path unfamiliar would cost them more than it saved, so improvements for new users could not come at the experts' expense.

03
No research practice to inherit Read more

There was no existing usability testing or research cadence on the product, so the evidence for every recommendation had to be produced alongside the design work rather than pulled from a backlog of findings.

04

Getting To Know Our Users And Competitors

Auditing Vantage Against Nielsen's Heuristics

Before proposing anything, I evaluated the shipping product against Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics, logging each violation with a recommendation, a severity rating and a screenshot. It was the cheapest way to build a shared, evidence-backed problem list with engineering and product.

  • 12 violations documented across 8 of the 10 heuristics
  • 5 rated major — including no confirmation on destructive deletes, editable-looking affordances in read-only views, and error states that disappeared before users could read them
  • Recognition rather than recall and consistency and standards were the worst-served heuristics, and became the spine of the redesign

Talking To The People Who Build Workflows

Interviews with broadcast and media workflow engineers let us test our assumptions about how workflows actually get built — who asks for them, what gets configured first, and where people give up and call support.

Who We Were Designing For

Our primary persona is the broadcast and media workflow engineer: highly technical, responsible for keeping live output error-free, and measured on downtime they cannot afford. Their goals were about reducing production time and manual work — their pain points were time-consuming, error-prone manual workflows and the difficulty of integrating and optimising the tools they already had.

What The Journey Mapping Revealed

Mapping a veteran engineer building a new transcoding workflow — from becoming aware of the requirement through to reviewing it in production — put the friction in the middle of the journey rather than at either end.

  • Gathering requirements — balancing flexibility against specificity when configuring Vantage
  • Research — no real-world examples to work from, so users leaned on forums and support
  • Building — integration risk and a learning curve steep enough to threaten downtime

What The Competitive Landscape Confirmed

Vantage was strong where it mattered and absent where users start. Benchmarking it against general-purpose workflow automation tools showed the gaps clustered around getting started and staying organised — templates, reuse, search, permissions, community — rather than around processing capability.

05

Translating Research Into A Redesigned Workflow Designer

Separating Workflows From The Design Canvas

The workflow list and the design canvas shared one screen with no hierarchy between them. Giving workflows a landing page of their own put status, ownership and failures in front of the user before they open anything.

Type-ahead search, category filtering and active / inactive / archived / failed tabs replaced a flat, unsearchable list.

Job failure counts sit inline on every row, so the workflows that need attention surface without anyone going looking for them.

Surfacing Every Action Instead Of Hiding It

The audit's clearest finding was recognition over recall: actions buried behind right-clicks became a searchable, categorised panel.

Users can favourite the actions they reach for constantly, and the panel filters by most recently used.

An empty state offers a starting point — Watch, Receive or Capture — so a blank canvas is no longer a dead end for a first-time user.

Making 100-Action Workflows Navigable

Workflows can run to a hundred actions with no way to manage or find any of them. The outline panel turns the canvas into a searchable list you can navigate.

Selecting an action in the outline moves the board to it, and configurations open from the outline rather than from a stack of modals.

Actions in an error state or missing required configuration are flagged in the outline, so problems are visible before the workflow is activated rather than after a job fails.

Turning Finished Workflows Into Reusable Templates

Vantage only allowed single-action templates, so teams saved whole workflows as stand-in templates and cluttered their categories. Now users can select or drag any group of actions and save it as a template.

Templates live in their own library, reachable from the side navigation and dragged straight onto the canvas.

A dedicated management view in Settings means templates can no longer be edited or duplicated by accident.

Where The Product Goes Next

The redesign covered the paths users hit first and most often. The backlog it produced is what comes after:

  • Usability testing as a standing practice — building testing into the UX process rather than validating after the fact
  • UI patterns for every action — so the rest of Vantage's action library can be ported onto the new system
  • A settings and management page for the configuration that currently has no home
  • Versioning history, the clearest gap the competitive analysis surfaced
  • Vantage error states, closing out the diagnose-and-recover findings from the heuristic audit
  • Job monitoring updates and the ability to switch between spaces
08

My Biggest Takeaways

A heuristic audit buys credibility cheaply

With no research practice to inherit, evaluating the shipping product against Nielsen's heuristics gave me an evidence-backed problem list in days — and gave engineering and product something concrete to argue with, rather than a designer's opinion.

Design for the new user without taxing the expert

Every change that made Vantage more discoverable had to leave the expert's path intact. Keeping Flip 64's familiar layout while modernising it was worth more than a cleaner redesign would have been.

Constraints are part of the brief

The back end generated each action's UI, so structural change was expensive. Designing within that shape, instead of around it, is what made the work shippable rather than aspirational.

AI earns its place when it removes expertise, not effort

Generating a workflow from a prompt mattered because the barrier was domain knowledge, not typing. Pairing it with an explicit preview-and-accept step is what kept it trustworthy in a system where a bad workflow costs airtime.
