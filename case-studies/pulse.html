Cutting no-shows by rewriting one reminder

Eleven percent of appointments went unfilled. The fix wasn't a louder notification — it was understanding why patients were saying yes to a reminder and still not showing up.

Role Product Designer
Timeline 5 weeks
Team 1 designer, 2 engineers, 1 PM
Tools Figma, Maze, Amplitude
Overview

A metric everyone tracked and no one owned

No-show rate showed up in every leadership deck, but it belonged to no single team — scheduling blamed reminders, reminders blamed patients, and the product itself hadn't changed the appointment reminder flow in over two years. I was asked to take a fresh look at a feature that seemed too simple to be the problem.

The problem

Confirmation wasn't the same as commitment

The existing reminder asked patients to confirm their appointment, and most did — confirmation rates were above 90%. But confirming a text message and actually restructuring your afternoon around a doctor's visit turned out to be two very different acts of commitment, and the product treated them as the same signal.

  • The reminder arrived too early to matter. A single message 48 hours out gave patients no reason to act on it in the moment.
  • Confirming felt costless. Tapping "yes" took less effort than actually canceling, so patients defaulted to confirming even when unsure.
  • Rescheduling was buried. The path to changing an appointment was harder to find than the path to silently not showing up.
Research & discovery

Talking to the patients who confirmed and still didn't come

  • Pulled six months of no-show data and cross-referenced it against confirmation timestamps to find the pattern: most no-shows had confirmed, most had confirmed early.
  • Recruited and interviewed 22 patients who'd no-showed in the past quarter, focused on what changed between confirming and the appointment day.
  • Ran a lightweight diary study with 10 patients across a full reminder-to-appointment cycle to capture real-time friction, not recalled friction.
  • Mapped the emotional register of each touchpoint — where the flow felt transactional versus where it felt like it was actually about their health.
Approach

Make the reminder do less confirming, more deciding

  • Moved from one reminder to a short sequence — an early heads-up, a day-of nudge, and a two-hour window check-in, each asking a different question instead of repeating the same one.
  • Replaced the binary confirm with a real choice: keep it, move it, or cancel it — surfaced with equal visual weight so rescheduling wasn't the hidden option.
  • Tested copy tone directly against no-show rate, not just click-through, since the two didn't move together in early experiments.
The solution

What shipped

A three-message reminder sequence with a one-tap reschedule path built into every message, rolled out gradually across clinics with an A/B test held against the original single-reminder flow to isolate the actual effect of the redesign from seasonal variation.

Impact

What changed after launch

11% → 6.2% no-show rate across test clinics
3.1× increase in self-service reschedules vs. cancellations
22 patient interviews behind the redesign
5 wks from research kickoff to A/B test launch
Reflection

The three-message sequence worked, but I'd want more data on whether the third message (the two-hour check-in) was pulling its weight versus just adding noise — the A/B test wasn't structured to isolate individual messages within the sequence, only the sequence as a whole against the original.
