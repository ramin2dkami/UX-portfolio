Pulse

Taking Real-Time Broadcast Monitoring Off The Rack

Pulse brings Telestream’s IP video, timing, and alarm monitoring into a single web application. I led the design of an experience that lets engineers see and configure their hardware from any computer, while everything stays on the customer’s own secure network.

01

Every Answer Lived At The Rack

Telestream’s IP monitoring lived in two places: Inspect 2110, a browser-based probe interface, and PRISM, a waveform monitor whose interface ran on the hardware itself. To check a signal or change a configuration, engineers often had to be standing at the device. Pulse set out to bring these capabilities into one web application that connects to the customer’s hardware and runs entirely on their own servers and network, with no internet connection, so sensitive media and data never leave the facility.

Design Problem

Critical signal data was trapped behind the hardware

  • Viewing or configuring a device meant going to the device.
  • Monitoring was split across Inspect 2110 and PRISM.
  • Dense tables gave every metric the same visual weight.
  • Alarms signaled a problem without the context to act on it.
  • Interfaces were organized around specifications, not tasks.
Business Problem

Customers needed remote visibility without exposing their network

  • Engineers work across control rooms, equipment rooms, and sites.
  • Security policies keep media networks isolated from the internet.
  • Two products meant two learning curves and two roadmaps.
  • Facilities moving to ST 2110 needed a modern IP monitoring toolset.
  • Hardware-bound interfaces limited how far a single unit could scale.
02

Two Powerful Tools, Built Around The Hardware

Before talking to customers, I reviewed the Inspect 2110 and PRISM interfaces that Pulse would replace. Both were powerful, but they were built around the device rather than the person using it. I treated what I found as hypotheses to test in customer interviews.

Key Learnings
01

The data lived at the device

PRISM’s touchscreen interface meant that diagnosing an issue often started with walking to the rack.

02

Everything carried equal weight

Uniform tables of packet counts and stream parameters made the one value that mattered look like every other.

03

Alarms stopped at “something is wrong”

A red Critical banner flagged a problem, but not which stream, when it started, or what to check next.

I asked customers how they monitor live IP signals today, where they lose time during an incident, and what they would need to trust a browser-based tool with on-air monitoring. The same needs kept surfacing across very different roles, and each one became a design opportunity that shaped a part of Pulse.

What Kept Coming Up

  1. 01
    What we heard

    See every device from one place

    Engineers pieced together a signal’s health from the rack, Inspect, and separate tools that didn’t integrate.

    →
    Design opportunity

    A configurable dashboard of live video, audio, waveform, and vector tiles, sourced from any connected device.

  2. 02
    What we heard

    Separate critical alarms from noise

    Too many alerts looked urgent, so engineers lost time during an incident working out which ones really were.

    →
    Design opportunity

    A severity-first alarm summary with counts, filters, acknowledgment, and grouped occurrences, shown beside the data it relates to.

  3. 03
    What we heard

    Bridge IT and broadcast data

    Network specialists lacked media context in their IT tools, while broadcast engineers lacked network context.

    →
    Design opportunity

    An input view that pairs video, audio, and ANC vitals with IP sessions, SFP details, and timing on the same page.

  4. 04
    What we heard

    Diagnose timing issues over time

    Multicast and PTP problems came and went, and were hard to understand from a single snapshot of values.

    →
    Design opportunity

    Per-port timelines, a live and historical time range, and a clear record of when each value last changed.

  5. 05
    What we heard

    Share context during incidents

    Findings lived in people’s heads, and incomplete documentation meant the next person started the investigation over.

    →
    Design opportunity

    Comments pinned to a moment on a graph, plus CSV and image export for reports and handoffs.

  6. 06
    What we heard

    Stay inside the secure network

    Protecting sensitive media and data was non-negotiable, so remote access couldn’t mean sending anything to the cloud.

    →
    Design opportunity

    A browser-based experience deployed on the customer’s own servers, with no internet dependency.

The takeaway

Give engineers the whole picture from any desk, without leaving their secure network.

Engineers didn’t want less data. They wanted to see every signal in one place, know which problems mattered, and understand them quickly, on both the broadcast and network side.

The Engineers Behind Every Broadcast

Personas

I synthesized the interviews into three personas that anchored design decisions across the product: two primary personas who share responsibility for the signal and the network that carries it, and a secondary persona focused on how the picture looks on air.

Primary Persona Broadcast and media workflow engineers Responsible for error-free live output and measured on downtime. They needed to resolve problems quickly, without being tied to a single piece of equipment.

Drake keeps live broadcasts running. His goals center on minimizing downtime and glitches during live events. His pain points were frequent equipment issues during broadcasts, time-consuming manual workflows, and difficulty integrating the many tools he already relied on.

Primary Persona IT and IP network specialists Responsible for the network that carries the media. They needed packet loss, latency, multicast, and PTP data in one place, alongside the broadcast context their IT tools could not show.

Edna keeps the facility network healthy under pressure. Her pain points were fragmented tools that did not integrate, alerts that were not actually critical, limited visibility into media-specific traffic, and hard-to-diagnose multicast and timing issues.

Secondary Persona Video shaders and colorists Responsible for how every frame looks on air. They relied on waveform and vector scopes and needed advanced HDR analysis without a complicated interface.

Daniel paints and balances cameras during live events and grades content afterward. His needs centered on real-time waveform and vector tools, and his pain points were unintuitive HDR tooling and balancing creative decisions against technical standards.

05

Bringing The Rack Into The Browser

I translated the research into four connected areas of the product, all built on a shared navigation and design language. Together, they let engineers build the view they need, dig into any input or timing source, and move from an alarm to its cause without leaving the browser.

Configurable Dashboards

PRISM showed one scope at a time on the hardware’s own screen. The Pulse dashboard turns that into a grid of live tiles that engineers arrange, configure, and group for the way their facility works.

Research signal

Engineers needed to watch many sources and scopes at once, from anywhere.

Design response

A grid-based dashboard of video and scope tiles, configured in a side panel and saved per team.

Key features

Grid layouts · Scope tiles · Tile presets · Color-coded groups · Saved dashboards

01

Grid-Based Layouts

Start from an empty state or insert a grid, then add tiles by right-clicking or using Add Tile.

02

Video And Scope Tiles

Picture, waveform, lightning, and stop display tiles bring PRISM’s scopes into the browser, side by side.

03

In-Context Tile Settings

A side panel organizes input, settings, display, and statistics into tabs, with presets that can be saved and reused.

04

Color-Coded Tile Groups

Select several tiles to assign a group color and a shared source, so related views change together.

05

Saved And Shared Dashboards

Save, favorite, share, and import dashboards so each team keeps the views that match its role.

Input Monitor

Inspect 2110 presented each stream as long tables of equally weighted metrics. The Input Monitor starts with a status-first overview of every input, then opens into tabs that pair signal vitals with the network details behind them.

Research signal

Broadcast and IP specialists each saw only half of the picture.

Design response

Input cards that show health at a glance, and a detail view that connects signal, network, and alarms.

Key features

Status cards · Guided setup · Vitals · IP sessions · In-context alarms

01

Status-First Input Cards

Each input shows a preview, its format, color-coded status, and alarm icons, plus clear states such as Disabled or Waiting for NMOS.

02

Guided Input Creation

One form covers the resource, standard, alarm profiles, and 2110 streams, with live previews of both paths as you configure.

03

Vitals At A Glance

Signal type, redundancy, and video, audio, and ANC details are summarized first, and mismatches link straight to the stream that caused them.

04

Network Detail Alongside Signal

IP sessions show SFP vendor details, link speed, and optical power for each network, as data, graphs, or both.

05

Alarms In Context

An alarm panel sits beside the input, so engineers can acknowledge issues without losing their place in the data.

PTP Monitor

Timing problems are often intermittent, so a single snapshot rarely explains them. The PTP Monitor shows lock status and alarm distribution up front, then lets engineers trace each port’s behavior over time.

Research signal

Multicast and timing issues were among the hardest to diagnose.

Design response

Status cards for each PTP resource and time-based graphs that show exactly when and where things changed.

Key features

Monitor cards · Alarm setup · Port timelines · Live and history · Collapsible graphs

01

PTP Monitor Cards

Each resource shows its domain, lock status (Locked, Unlocked, or Drifting), and a bar of active alarms by severity.

02

Alarm Setup At Creation

Engineers choose which PTP alarms to track, and set their duration and severity, while creating the monitor.

03

Port-By-Port Timelines

State bars show when a port moved from Follower to Listening or Faulty, with hover details for each change.

04

Live And Historical Views

A date picker and interval control let engineers look back at an incident, then return to live data with Go Live.

05

Collapsible Graphs

Every metric shows its current value and last change, and its graph expands only when it is needed.

Alarms, Events, And Collaboration

In the legacy tools, an alarm was a red banner. In Pulse, every alarm is a record that can be filtered, investigated, acknowledged, and discussed, with the event log and graph comments preserving what the team learned.

Research signal

Alarm noise and lost context slowed resolution and handoffs.

Design response

A severity-first alarm summary, a searchable event log, and comments attached directly to graphs.

Key features

Severity tabs · Alarm details · Drill-down · Event log · Graph comments

01

Severity-First Summary

Tabs with counts for Critical, Warning, and Informational alarms help engineers focus on what matters first.

02

Alarm Details Panel

Selecting an alarm shows its source, device, duration, occurrences, and a video thumbnail without leaving the table.

03

Drill-Down And Acknowledge

Engineers can view every occurrence of an issue, then acknowledge one alarm or many in bulk.

04

Searchable Event Log

Every event is timestamped, filterable by date range, and exportable, with timecode and device details for each entry.

05

Comments On Graphs

Engineers pin comments to a moment on a graph, so the next person sees what happened and what was already checked.

Putting Pulse In Front Of Engineers

Each feature area went through its own round of unmoderated usability testing in UserZoom, built on the same template: a small group of participants, a set of realistic tasks, and three measures. I linked a clickable prototype into each UserZoom study, and participants worked through the tasks on their own while UserZoom recorded their screen, voice, and click paths. After each task, UserZoom asked participants whether they had completed it, which gave me perceived completion. I then reviewed the recordings and click paths to score actual completion. Each study ended with a System Usability Scale survey, also delivered in UserZoom. The gap between perceived and actual completion was the most useful signal, because it showed where engineers felt confident but were quietly going wrong.

What Pulse Taught Me

01

Hierarchy beats more screens

Status colors, severity counts, and “last changed” timestamps did more to speed up scanning than adding new views ever would have.

02

Connect the two expert views

Broadcast and IP specialists look at the same incident from different sides. Putting signal and network data on one page gave them a shared picture.

03

Watch the perception gap

Participants felt successful more often than they were. Measuring actual completion alongside SUS caught problems a satisfaction score alone would have hidden.

What I delivered
  • End-to-end design for Dashboards, Input Monitor, PTP Monitor, and Alarms, all of which shipped.
  • A secure, on-premise web experience that replaced hardware-only monitoring.
  • A shared navigation and design language across the product.
  • Customer interviews, three personas, and usability testing for every feature area.
What I’d measure next
  • Time from alarm to root cause.
  • How often engineers still need to visit the rack.
  • Dashboard adoption and reuse across teams.
